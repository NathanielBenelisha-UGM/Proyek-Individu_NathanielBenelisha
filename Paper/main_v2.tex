\begin{abstract}
The rapid proliferation of Inverter-Based Resources (IBR)---including
solar photovoltaic (PV), wind turbines (WT), and Battery Energy
Storage Systems (BESS)---reduces natural rotational inertia in modern
power systems, exacerbating initial Rate of Change of Frequency (RoCoF)
and challenging Primary Frequency Regulation (PFR) following N-1
generation contingencies.
This paper proposes an enhanced \textit{Frequency-Constrained Unit
Commitment} (FCUC) model that co-optimizes conventional generator
scheduling, renewable generation (solar PV and wind), Primary Frequency
Regulation, and dedicated Virtual Inertia (VI) provision from BESS
within a unified Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) framework
implemented in Python~3.9/Pyomo and solved via IBM CPLEX~22.1.1.
Unlike conventional setups, virtual inertia is supplied strictly by
grid-forming BESS, incorporating converter power allocation coupling
between bulk energy discharge and virtual inertia headroom, while
wind turbines and solar PV operate as energy-supplying generators with
curtailment capabilities. The PFR formulation accounts for governor
droop characteristics, ramp-rate limits, and steady-state frequency
deviation constraints derived from the Southern Sulawesi power system
framework.
Seven simulation scenarios on a modified IEEE 10-unit test system
with a RoCoF threshold of 0.55~Hz/s are comprehensively evaluated.
The unconstrained classical UC (Scenario~1) yields \$4{,}029{,}223/day
but violates RoCoF in all 24 scheduling periods (worst-case 0.903~Hz/s).
Enforcing inertia security using synchronous generators alone (Scenario~4)
guarantees frequency compliance but incurs a $+$3.65\% cost penalty
(\$4{,}176{,}358/day). Incorporating BESS virtual inertia (Scenario~6)
resolves all RoCoF violations while lowering operating cost to
\$3{,}938{,}983/day (a 5.70\% savings relative to Scenario~4 and 2.24\%
below baseline UC). These findings confirm that co-optimizing BESS
virtual inertia and PFR ensures dynamic frequency security without
requiring the costly over-commitment of thermal units.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Motivation and Background}

Indonesia's State Electricity Company (PLN) National Electricity
Supply Business Plan (RUPTL) 2025--2034 targets 69.5~GW of new
generation capacity, with 61\% (${\approx}42.4$~GW) from renewable
energy sources~\cite{PLN2025}. This mirrors a global trend in which
inverter-interfaced generation increasingly displaces conventional
synchronous machines (SG), introducing the \emph{inertia deficit
problem}~\cite{Tielens2016}.

Unlike SGs, which store kinetic energy in rotating masses and
release it instantaneously to arrest frequency deviations, IBR
are decoupled from the grid via power-electronic converters and
contribute no natural inertia. As IBR penetration grows, total
system inertia decreases, causing:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Higher RoCoF}: Inversely proportional to total
    system inertia $H_{\text{sys}}$; can trigger protective relay
    operation and cascading failures~\cite{Tielens2016};
  \item \textbf{Deeper frequency nadir}: The minimum frequency
    reached may fall below UFLS thresholds (49.0~Hz in Indonesia);
  \item \textbf{Degraded quasi-steady-state (QSS) frequency}: If
    insufficient governor capacity is committed, the settled
    post-disturbance frequency may be inadequate.
\end{enumerate}

These risks have been demonstrated in major incidents: the 2016
South Australia blackout and the 2019 Great Britain blackout both
resulted from low-inertia conditions~\cite{AEMO2017}.
Indonesia's isolated grid systems (Sulawesi, Kalimantan, Maluku,
Papua) with rapidly growing renewable portfolios are increasingly
exposed to similar risks~\cite{E02Sulawesi,E03JAMALI}.

\subsection{Literature Review}

\subsubsection{Unit Commitment --- Definition and History}

Unit Commitment (UC) is a fundamental optimization problem in power
system operations that determines the optimal on/off schedule
(\textit{commitment}) of generating units over a planning horizon
--- typically 24 to 168 hours --- to minimize total operating costs
while satisfying load demand and operational
constraints~\cite{Padhy2004}. Formally, UC is a large-scale
combinatorial optimization problem involving binary decision variables
(unit status $u_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$), continuous variables (power
output $P_{i,t}$), and constraints including power balance, generation
capacity limits, ramp rates, minimum up/down times, and spinning
reserve requirements~\cite{Conejo2010}.

The UC problem was first recognized through the work of
Baldwin \textit{et al.} (1959) on economic shutdown of generating
units, marking the transition from manual scheduling toward
systematic optimization~\cite{Padhy2004}. Since then, UC has become
one of the most extensively studied optimization problems in power
engineering, with hundreds of publications comprehensively
categorized in the bibliographical survey by Padhy~\cite{Padhy2004}.

\subsubsection{Evolution of UC Solution Methods}

Solution techniques for UC have evolved significantly over the
past six decades~\cite{Padhy2004}.

\textbf{Priority List (PL)} is the earliest and simplest approach:
units are ranked by fuel cost efficiency and committed sequentially
until demand and reserve requirements are met. While computationally
fast, PL often yields suboptimal solutions because it ignores
inter-temporal coupling constraints.

\textbf{Dynamic Programming (DP)} provided the first rigorous
mathematical framework, decomposing the combinatorial UC into
smaller staged sub-problems. However, DP suffers from the
\textit{curse of dimensionality} --- computational complexity
grows exponentially with the number of generating units, making
it impractical for large-scale systems.

\textbf{Lagrangian Relaxation (LR)} became the historically
dominant method for large-scale UC. By relaxing binding constraints
(e.g., power balance) into the objective function via Lagrange
multipliers, LR decomposes the problem into per-unit sub-problems
that are individually tractable, providing a lower bound on the
optimal cost.

\textbf{Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP)} is now the
dominant approach in modern power system operations, enabled by
advances in commercial solvers such as IBM CPLEX and
Gurobi~\cite{Carrion2006}. Carri\'on and Arroyo~\cite{Carrion2006}
proposed a computationally efficient MILP formulation for thermal
UC requiring fewer binary variables and constraints than earlier
methods; this formulation became the standard benchmark.
MILP models unit on/off decisions as binary variables and
operational constraints as linear inequalities, guaranteeing
global optimality within a specified tolerance gap.
Conejo \textit{et al.}~\cite{Conejo2010} further extended this
framework for decision-making under uncertainty in electricity
markets, including stochastic UC formulations.

\subsubsection{Low-Inertia Power System Challenges}

Rotational inertia is a fundamental physical property of
synchronous generators (SG): kinetic energy stored in the spinning
rotor mass provides an instantaneous frequency arresting response
following power imbalances~\cite{Kundur1994}. As reviewed by
Tielens and Van~Hertem~\cite{Tielens2016}, when SGs are displaced
by IBR --- solar PV, wind turbines, and BESS --- system inertia
declines proportionally because IBR are decoupled from the grid
via power-electronic converters.

The consequences of reduced inertia are characterized by the
System Frequency Response (SFR) model~\cite{Anderson1990}, a
second-order transfer function approximating the closed-loop
frequency dynamics. Anderson and Mirheydar~\cite{Anderson1990}
derived three key frequency metrics from this model:
(i)~RoCoF $= f_0 \Delta P_{\text{loss}} / (2 H_{\text{sys}})$,
inversely proportional to system inertia;
(ii)~frequency nadir, the maximum deviation; and
(iii)~quasi-steady-state (QSS) deviation after primary response
completes.

The severity of low-inertia risks has been demonstrated in
major incidents: the 2016 South Australia blackout (loss of
850~MW; AEMO identified low inertia as a key contributing
factor~\cite{AEMO2017}) and the 2019 Great Britain blackout
(1{,}131~MW loss causing frequency to fall to 48.8~Hz, triggering
UFLS disconnecting ${>}$1~million customers).

\subsubsection{Frequency-Constrained Unit Commitment}

The FCUC concept addresses the fundamental limitation of classical
UC: cost-optimal schedules produced without frequency dynamics
consideration can be \textit{frequency-insecure} when system inertia
is low. FCUC incorporates frequency security constraints (RoCoF,
nadir, QSS) directly into the optimization formulation.

The development of FCUC has progressed chronologically as follows:

\textbf{Ahmadi and Ghasemi (2014)}~\cite{Ahmadi2014} formulated
security-constrained UC with linearized system frequency limit
constraints, demonstrating that linear approximations of the nadir
constraint enable solution via standard MILP solvers.

\textbf{Teng, Trovato, and Strbac (2016)}~\cite{Teng2016} proposed
stochastic scheduling with inertia-dependent fast frequency response
(FFR) requirements. Their model showed that FFR requirements increase
non-linearly as system inertia decreases, establishing the
relationship between scheduling decisions and frequency security.

\textbf{Wen, Li, Huang, and Liu (2016)}~\cite{Wen2016} proposed the
first MILP-based FCUC explicitly embedding RoCoF and nadir
constraints derived from the SFR model~\cite{Anderson1990}, with
BESS as a fast-acting frequency responder. This seminal paper
established the framework for integrating BESS into FCUC.

\textbf{Xu, Jang, and Overbye (2018)}~\cite{Xu2018} extended this
concept with commitment of fast-responding storage devices to mimic
inertia, enhancing primary frequency response without requiring
additional synchronous generator commitment.

\textbf{Chu, Markovic, Hug, and Teng (2020)}~\cite{Chu2020} proposed
a stochastic UC model incorporating synthetic inertia provision
from wind turbines via a two-stage control framework. Their key
innovation was treating wind turbine synthetic inertia as a
\textit{dispatchable ancillary service} and eliminating the
\textit{secondary frequency dip} problem through careful rotor
speed recovery management.

\textbf{Asiedu \textit{et al.} (2025)}~\cite{Asiedu2025} provided
a comprehensive review of dynamic constraint integration into
steady-state optimization models for IBR-dominated systems,
identifying key research gaps and open problems.

\subsubsection{Virtual Inertia: Concepts and Technologies}

Virtual inertia (VI) is a control strategy implemented in
power-electronic converters to emulate the inertial response and
damping characteristics of a synchronous generator. By integrating
energy storage (batteries, DC-link capacitors) with specialized
control loops, grid-forming inverters can autonomously inject or
absorb power during frequency deviations, slowing RoCoF and
improving nadir.

\textbf{BESS Virtual Inertia.} BESS provides VI through
grid-forming converter control: the converter responds to frequency
deviations by injecting/absorbing power from the battery. BESS
offers sub-millisecond response time and bidirectional operation
capability. Optimal placement of ESS-based VI for frequency
stability has been studied by Fang \textit{et al.}~\cite{VI06OptimalPlacement}.

\textbf{Wind Turbine Virtual Inertia.} Modern wind turbines (DFIG
and PMSG types) store kinetic energy in the rotating rotor mass.
Synthetic inertia is obtained by transiently extracting rotor
kinetic energy when frequency drops, then gradually recovering
rotor speed. The primary challenge is the \textit{secondary
frequency dip} that occurs during rotor speed recovery;
Chu \textit{et al.}~\cite{Chu2020} addressed this through their
two-stage control framework.

\subsubsection{Linearization of Frequency Nadir Constraint}

The frequency nadir constraint is inherently nonlinear --- it is a
function of $H_{\text{sys}}$, $R_{\text{sys}}$,
$\Delta P_{\text{loss}}$, and damping parameters. To maintain MILP
tractability, linearization is essential.

Shi \textit{et al.}~\cite{Shi2025} proposed a two-stage
linearization method: Stage~1 maps commitment decisions linearly
to system frequency parameters ($H_{\text{sys}}, R_{\text{sys}}$);
Stage~2 approximates the nadir as a linear function of these
parameters via multivariate regression. This approach enables
tractable MILP formulation with high accuracy.
Liu \textit{et al.}~\cite{Liu2024} further addressed the nadir
constraint for high-penetration island power systems where
system inertia fluctuates significantly.

\subsubsection{Indonesian Power System Context}

Indonesia's RUPTL 2025--2034~\cite{PLN2025} targets 61\%
renewable generation, requiring massive IBR integration that will
significantly reduce system inertia --- particularly in isolated
grids (Sulawesi, Kalimantan, Maluku, Papua).

UC with primary frequency regulation in the Southern Sulawesi
isolated grid~\cite{E02Sulawesi} demonstrated a 7.5\% cost
increase when PFR constraints are enforced, but enabled BESS to
extend VRE hosting capacity to 36--41\% of peak load.
A complementary study on the Jawa-Madura-Bali (JAMALI)
system~\cite{E03JAMALI} incorporated a 2-minute FFR constraint,
requiring 675~MW FFR with only a 0.3\% cost increase.
Nguyen-Hong and Nakanishi~\cite{D02ForecastError} examined FCUC
considering BESS and renewable forecast error, highlighting the
importance of uncertainty in UC scheduling.

\subsubsection{Research Gaps}

Despite the extensive body of literature reviewed above, several
critical research gaps remain:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{(G1)} Existing FCUC formulations often omit explicit
    converter power allocation coupling between BESS energy arbitrage
    discharge and virtual inertia headroom, risking physical converter
    overloading during contingency response;
  \item \textbf{(G2)} The integration of rigorous governor-droop
    Primary Frequency Regulation (PFR) constraints---guaranteeing
    quasi-steady-state frequency nadir and steady-state compliance
    following N-1 outages---is rarely co-optimized with BESS virtual
    inertia and high VRE penetration (wind and solar PV) in a tractable
    MILP framework;
  \item \textbf{(G3)} Detailed empirical validation and systematic
    cost--security trade-off assessments tailored to Indonesian power
    grid characteristics (such as the Southern Sulawesi interconnected
    system under RUPTL 2025--2034 renewable targets) remain scarce.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions}

This paper addresses the identified gaps through:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{BESS Virtual Inertia and PFR FCUC Formulation:}
    A rigorous MILP FCUC model co-optimizing thermal generator dispatch,
    dedicated BESS virtual inertia with explicit discharge power
    allocation coupling, governor-droop Primary Frequency Regulation
    (PFR) based on the Southern Sulawesi grid formulation~\cite{E02Sulawesi},
    and renewable generation from solar PV and wind turbines;

  \item \textbf{Seven-Scenario Systematic Analysis:} Evaluation across
    seven constraint configurations (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios})
    quantifies the cost--security trade-off, demonstrating how BESS
    virtual inertia effectively eliminates RoCoF violations while
    lowering operational expenditures;

  \item \textbf{Practical Insights for Indonesian Power Systems:}
    Case study parameters and operational constraints aligned with
    Indonesian grid codes and RUPTL 2025--2034 targets, confirming that
    frequency-secure operation can be achieved without inflating total
    system costs.
\end{enumerate}

\section{System Modeling}
\label{sec:model}

This section formulates the mathematical models for generating resources, battery storage, renewable generation, system inertia, and primary frequency regulation (PFR) in accordance with the Southern Sulawesi power system operational framework~\cite{E02Sulawesi} and established frequency stability theory.

\subsection{Conventional Thermal Generator Model}

Consider an interconnected power system with $N$ conventional thermal generating units indexed by $g \in \mathcal{G} = \{1, 2, \dots, N\}$ over a scheduling horizon of $T$ hourly intervals indexed by $t \in \mathcal{T} = \{1, 2, \dots, T\}$. 

The operational generation cost of thermal unit~$g$ at time~$t$, denoted as $F_g(P_{g,t})$, is represented by an affine function of active power output~\cite{E02Sulawesi}:
\begin{equation}
  F_g(P_{g,t}) = a_g\,u_{g,t} + b_g\,P_{g,t}
  \label{eq:thermal_cost}
\end{equation}
where $u_{g,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ is the binary commitment status of unit~$g$ at period~$t$ ($u_{g,t}=1$ if committed/online, and $0$ if offline), $P_{g,t}$ is the active power generation output [MW], $a_g$ is the fixed (no-load) operational cost [\$], and $b_g$ is the marginal fuel cost coefficient [\$/MWh].

Each committed synchronous generator contributes physical rotational kinetic energy stored within its rotating turbine-generator mass. With inertia constant $H_g$~[s or MWs/MVA] and maximum capacity rating $P_g^{\max}$~[MW], the aggregate natural rotational inertia provided by online thermal units at period~$t$, denoted as $H_{\text{thermal},t}$~[MWs], is given by:
\begin{equation}
  H_{\text{thermal},t} = \sum_{g=1}^{N} H_g\,P_g^{\max}\,u_{g,t}
  \label{eq:thermal_inertia}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) and Virtual Inertia Model}

The energy storage fleet comprises $B$ BESS installations indexed by $b \in \mathcal{B} = \{1, 2, \dots, B\}$. The State of Charge $\text{SOC}_{b,t}$~[MWh] tracks the usable electrical energy stored in battery unit~$b$ at time~$t$. The discrete-time SOC dynamic transition over hourly intervals ($\Delta t = 1$~h) is governed by:
\begin{equation}
  \text{SOC}_{b,t} = 
  \begin{cases}
    \text{SOC}_b^{\text{init}} + \eta_b^{\text{ch}}\,P_{b,1}^{\text{ch}} - \dfrac{P_{b,1}^{\text{dis}}}{\eta_b^{\text{dis}}}, & t = 1 \\[10pt]
    \text{SOC}_{b,t-1} + \eta_b^{\text{ch}}\,P_{b,t}^{\text{ch}} - \dfrac{P_{b,t}^{\text{dis}}}{\eta_b^{\text{dis}}}, & \forall\,t \in \{2, \dots, T\}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:soc_dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $P_{b,t}^{\text{ch}}$ and $P_{b,t}^{\text{dis}}$ denote charging and discharging active power [MW], $\eta_b^{\text{ch}} \in (0, 1]$ and $\eta_b^{\text{dis}} \in (0, 1]$ are charge and discharge efficiencies, and $\text{SOC}_b^{\text{init}}$ is the initial energy level at $t=0$.

In addition to bulk energy arbitrage (charging during low-cost/high-VRE hours and discharging during peak demand), the BESS operates with grid-forming (GFM) converter controls to supply sub-second virtual inertia (VI). The equivalent virtual inertia constant $H_{\text{BESS},t}$~[MWs] contributed by the battery fleet is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
  H_{\text{BESS},t} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} K_b^{\text{VI}}\,P_{b,t}^{\text{VI}}
  \label{eq:bess_vi}
\end{equation}
where $K_b^{\text{VI}}$ represents the virtual inertia constant of battery unit~$b$~[s] and $P_{b,t}^{\text{VI}}$ is the active power headroom allocated specifically for virtual inertia support [MW].

\textbf{BESS Converter Power Allocation Coupling:}
A critical physical requirement in battery energy storage operation is ensuring that the power conversion system (PCS) rating is never exceeded during simultaneous energy dispatch and frequency response. Specifically, the power allocated for grid energy discharge $P_{b,t}^{\text{dis}}$ and the headroom reserved for virtual inertia $P_{b,t}^{\text{VI}}$ share the same converter capacity. Accounting for the converter efficiency during virtual inertia injection $\eta_b^{\text{VI}} \in (0, 1]$, the coupled discharge capability limit is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
  P_{b,t}^{\text{dis}} + \frac{P_{b,t}^{\text{VI}}}{\eta_b^{\text{VI}}} \leq DR_b^{\max}\,u_{b,t}^{\text{dis}}
  \label{eq:bess_power_alloc}
\end{equation}
where $u_{b,t}^{\text{dis}} \in \{0, 1\}$ is the binary discharging status variable, $DR_b^{\max}$ is the maximum continuous discharge power rating [MW], and $P_{b,t}^{\text{VI}} \geq 0$. Equation~\eqref{eq:bess_power_alloc} ensures that allocating battery capacity to virtual inertia directly constrains its simultaneous energy dispatch, preventing inverter overload.

\subsection{Renewable Generation Model (Wind Turbine and Solar PV)}

Renewable energy generation includes $W$ wind turbine (PLTB) farms indexed by $w \in \mathcal{W} = \{1, \dots, W\}$ and $V$ solar photovoltaic (PLTS) plants indexed by $v \in \mathcal{V} = \{1, \dots, V\}$.

For each wind farm~$w$, available generation $P_{w,t}^{\text{avail}}$~[MW] is determined by the number of active wind turbine units $N_w$, the rated capacity per turbine $P_w^{\text{rated}}$~[MW], and the normalized wind availability profile $\Phi_{W_t} \in [0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
  P_{w,t}^{\text{avail}} = N_w\,P_w^{\text{rated}}\,\Phi_{W_t}
  \label{eq:wt_avail}
\end{equation}
The dispatched wind generation $P_{w,t}^{\text{used}}$ injected into the grid equals available output minus curtailment $P_{w,t}^{\text{curt}}$:
\begin{equation}
  P_{w,t}^{\text{used}} = P_{w,t}^{\text{avail}} - P_{w,t}^{\text{curt}}, \quad 0 \leq P_{w,t}^{\text{curt}} \leq P_{w,t}^{\text{avail}}
  \label{eq:wt_used}
\end{equation}

Similarly, for each solar PV installation~$v$, available power $P_{v,t}^{\text{avail}}$~[MW] is given by rated capacity $N_v\,P_v^{\text{rated}}$ and solar irradiance potential $\Phi_{PV_t} \in [0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
  P_{v,t}^{\text{avail}} = N_v\,P_v^{\text{rated}}\,\Phi_{PV_t}
  \label{eq:pv_avail}
\end{equation}
The dispatched solar PV power injected into the power system is:
\begin{equation}
  P_{v,t}^{\text{used}} = P_{v,t}^{\text{avail}} - P_{v,t}^{\text{curt}}, \quad 0 \leq P_{v,t}^{\text{curt}} \leq P_{v,t}^{\text{avail}}
  \label{eq:pv_used}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Non-Participation of Renewables in Virtual Inertia:}
In this work, wind turbines and solar PV installations operate strictly as grid-following energy producers under maximum power point tracking with downward curtailment capability. To eliminate rotor deceleration stress, kinetic energy recovery dips, and de-loading revenue losses in wind generation, WT and PV do not contribute to virtual inertia ($P_{w,t}^{\text{VI}} = 0, P_{v,t}^{\text{VI}} = 0$). Synthetic inertia is therefore provided exclusively by the dedicated BESS.

\subsection{Aggregate System Inertia and RoCoF Dynamics}

The total effective system inertia at scheduling period~$t$, $H_{\text{sys},t}$~[MWs], is the sum of natural rotational inertia from synchronous generators and virtual inertia from BESS:
\begin{equation}
  H_{\text{sys},t} = \sum_{g=1}^{N} H_g\,P_g^{\max}\,u_{g,t} + \sum_{b=1}^{B} K_b^{\text{VI}}\,P_{b,t}^{\text{VI}}
  \label{eq:hsys_total}
\end{equation}
Notice that~\eqref{eq:hsys_total} is strictly linear in the decision variables $u_{g,t}$ and $P_{b,t}^{\text{VI}}$, ensuring full compatibility with Mixed-Integer Linear Programming.

Following a severe contingency event corresponding to the loss of the largest generation unit ($P^{\text{loss}}_t$~[MW]), the power mismatch instantaneously accelerates system frequency deviation according to the center-of-inertia (COI) swing equation~\cite{Anderson1990}:
\begin{equation}
  2\,H_{\text{sys},t}\,\left.\frac{df}{dt}\right|_{t=0^+} = -f_0\,P^{\text{loss}}_t
  \label{eq:swing}
\end{equation}
where $f_0$ is nominal frequency ($50$~Hz). The initial Rate of Change of Frequency ($\text{RoCoF}_t$) is given by:
\begin{equation}
  \text{RoCoF}_t = \left|\left.\frac{df}{dt}\right|_{t=0^+}\right| = \frac{f_0\,P^{\text{loss}}_t}{2\,H_{\text{sys},t}} \quad [\text{Hz/s}]
  \label{eq:rocof_def}
\end{equation}
To satisfy system security requirements and avoid tripping RoCoF protective relays, the RoCoF limit $\text{RoCoF}^{\max}$~[Hz/s] is enforced by ensuring sufficient system inertia:
\begin{equation}
  \text{RoCoF}_t \leq \text{RoCoF}^{\max} \iff H_{\text{sys},t} \geq \frac{f_0\,P^{\text{loss}}_t}{2\,\text{RoCoF}^{\max}}
  \label{eq:rocof_bound}
\end{equation}
where $P^{\text{loss}}_t = P_{k_t} = \max_{g \in \mathcal{G}} (P_{g,t}\,u_{g,t})$ or $\max_{g \in \mathcal{G}} (P_g^{\max}\,u_{g,t})$.

\subsection{Primary Frequency Regulation (PFR) Model}

Following inertial deceleration, primary frequency response takes effect over the interval $2 < t \leq 10$--$30$~s through autonomous turbine-governor action. The frequency settles at a quasi-steady-state value $f_{\text{ss},t} = f_0 + \Delta f_t$, where $\Delta f_t \leq 0$ is the steady-state frequency deviation resulting from the contingency.

In accordance with the Southern Sulawesi system study~\cite{E02Sulawesi} and Restrepo and Galiana~\cite{Restrepo2005}, thermal units with active free governors increase active power in proportion to frequency drop according to their speed droop characteristics. The primary reserve delivered by unit~$g$ at period~$t$, $R_{g,t}^{\text{PFR}}$~[MW], is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
  R_{g,t}^{\text{PFR}} = \min\left(-K_g\,\Delta f_t, \, P_{g,t}^{\max,\text{FR}} - P_{g,t}\right)
  \label{eq:pfr_droop}
\end{equation}
where $K_g = \frac{P_g^{\text{rated}}}{\sigma_g\,f_0}$ is the governor frequency bias (gain) of unit~$g$~[MW/Hz] with droop slope $\sigma_g$, and $P_{g,t}^{\max,\text{FR}}$ is the maximum power output deliverable within the primary regulation window:
\begin{equation}
  P_{g,t}^{\max,\text{FR}} = \min\left(P_g^{\max}\,u_{g,t}, \, P_{g,t} + R_g^{\text{PFR},\max}\,u_{g,t}\right)
  \label{eq:p_max_fr}
\end{equation}
Here, $R_g^{\text{PFR},\max} = v_g^{\text{gov}}\,\Delta t_{\text{PFR}}$ is the maximum primary reserve delivery capability determined by governor ramping speed $v_g^{\text{gov}}$~[MW/s] over primary regulation timeframe $\Delta t_{\text{PFR}} \approx 10$~s.

Equation~\eqref{eq:pfr_droop} defines two distinct operating regions:
\begin{equation}
  R_{g,t}^{\text{PFR}} = 
  \begin{cases}
    -K_g\,\Delta f_t, & \text{if } \Delta f_t \geq \Delta f_{g,t}^{\max} \\[6pt]
    -K_g\,\Delta f_{g,t}^{\max}, & \text{if } \Delta f_t \leq \Delta f_{g,t}^{\max}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:pfr_piecewise}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta f_{g,t}^{\max} \leq 0$ is the saturation frequency deviation at which generator~$g$ reaches its maximum headroom:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta f_{g,t}^{\max} = \frac{P_{g,t} - P_{g,t}^{\max,\text{FR}}}{K_g}
  \label{eq:delta_f_max}
\end{equation}
To avoid triggering under-frequency load shedding (UFLS) relays (set at $49.0$~Hz in Indonesia), steady-state frequency deviation under the worst-case contingency must remain above the permissible bound:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta f_t \geq \Delta f_{\text{ss}}^{\min} \quad (\text{e.g., } \Delta f_{\text{ss}}^{\min} = -0.5\text{ Hz} \implies f_{\text{ss},t} \geq 49.5\text{ Hz})
  \label{eq:ss_freq_limit}
\end{equation}

\section{FCUC Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

Integrating the operational models of conventional generators, BESS virtual inertia, renewable generation, and primary frequency regulation yields the comprehensive Frequency-Constrained Unit Commitment (FCUC) problem formulated as a Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP).

\subsection{Objective Function}

The objective of the optimization is to minimize the total system operational cost over the planning horizon $T$ (matching~\cite{E02Sulawesi} and Image~1):
\begin{equation}
  \min \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{g=1}^{N} \left[ u_{g,t}\,F_g(P_{g,t}) + SU_{g,t} \right]
  \label{eq:objective_base}
\end{equation}
Substituting the affine generation cost function~\eqref{eq:thermal_cost} and modeling startup cost via binary startup transition variables $v_{g,t} \in \{0, 1\}$:
\begin{equation}
  \min \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{g=1}^{N} \left[ a_g\,u_{g,t} + b_g\,P_{g,t} + SU_g\,v_{g,t} \right]
  \label{eq:objective_full}
\end{equation}
where $a_g$ is the fixed cost [\$], $b_g$ is the variable fuel cost [\$/MWh], and $SU_g$ is the startup cost [\$] of unit~$g$.

\subsection{Operational Constraints}

\subsubsection{C1: Active Power Balance}
Active power generated by thermal units, net BESS discharge, wind injection, and solar PV injection must satisfy hourly demand $P_{L,t}$~[MW] (Image~2 extended with solar PV):
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \sum_{g=1}^{N} P_{g,t}
    &+ \sum_{b=1}^{B} \left( P_{b,t}^{\text{dis}} - P_{b,t}^{\text{ch}} \right)
    + \sum_{w=1}^{W} \left( P_{w,t}^{\text{avail}} - P_{w,t}^{\text{curt}} \right) \\
    &+ \sum_{v=1}^{V} \left( P_{v,t}^{\text{avail}} - P_{v,t}^{\text{curt}} \right)
    = P_{L,t} \quad \forall\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:power_balance}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{C2: Thermal Generation Limits and Ramping}
Each conventional unit is constrained by technical capacity bounds and ramp-rate limits (Image~2 and~\cite{E02Sulawesi}):
\begin{align}
  P_g^{\min}\,u_{g,t} &\leq P_{g,t} \leq P_g^{\max}\,u_{g,t} \quad \forall\,g \in \mathcal{G},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:gen_limit} \\
  -RD_g &\leq P_{g,t} - P_{g,t-1} \leq RU_g \quad \forall\,g \in \mathcal{G},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:ramp_limit}
\end{align}
where $P_g^{\min}$ and $P_g^{\max}$ are minimum and maximum dispatch limits [MW], and $RU_g, RD_g$ are maximum hourly ramp-up and ramp-down rates [MW/h].

\subsubsection{C3: Minimum Up-Time and Down-Time}
To prevent thermal stress, generating units must adhere to minimum uptime $T_g^{\text{up}}$ and downtime $T_g^{\text{down}}$ (Image~3 and~\cite{E02Sulawesi}):
\begin{align}
  \sum_{\tau=t-T_g^{\text{up}}+1}^{t} v_{g,\tau} &\leq u_{g,t} \quad \forall\,g \in \mathcal{G},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:min_uptime} \\
  \sum_{\tau=t-T_g^{\text{down}}+1}^{t} w_{g,\tau} &\leq 1 - u_{g,t} \quad \forall\,g \in \mathcal{G},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:min_downtime} \\
  u_{g,t} - u_{g,t-1} &= v_{g,t} - w_{g,t} \quad \forall\,g \in \mathcal{G},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:status_logic} \\
  v_{g,t} + w_{g,t} &\leq 1 \quad \forall\,g \in \mathcal{G},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:no_sim_su_sd}
\end{align}
where $v_{g,t}, w_{g,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ are binary startup and shutdown decision variables.

\subsubsection{C4: Spinning Reserve and N-1 Contingency}
Scheduled spinning reserve $R_{g,t}$ is bounded by generator headroom, and aggregate spinning reserves must cover the single largest committed generator output $P_{k_t}$ (Image~3):
\begin{align}
  P_{g,t} + R_{g,t} &\leq P_g^{\max}\,u_{g,t} \quad \forall\,g \in \mathcal{G},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:reserve_headroom} \\
  \sum_{g \neq k} R_{g,t} &\geq P_{k_t} \quad \forall\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:n1_reserve}
\end{align}
where $P_{k_t} = \max_{g \in \mathcal{G}} (P_{g,t})$ is the dispatched output of the contingency unit.

\subsubsection{C5: BESS Operational Limits and Power Allocation}
The battery fleet satisfies energy storage limits, cyclic energy balance, charging/discharging limits, and converter power allocation coupling (Images~3, 4):
\begin{align}
  \text{SOC}_b^{\min} &\leq \text{SOC}_{b,t} \leq \text{SOC}_b^{\max} \quad \forall\,b \in \mathcal{B},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:soc_limits} \\
  \text{SOC}_{b,T} &= \text{SOC}_b^{\text{final}} \quad \forall\,b \in \mathcal{B}
  \label{eq:final_soc} \\
  P_{b,t}^{\text{ch}} &\leq CR_b^{\max}\,u_{b,t}^{\text{ch}} \quad \forall\,b \in \mathcal{B},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:ch_limit} \\
  u_{b,t}^{\text{ch}} + u_{b,t}^{\text{dis}} &\leq 1 \quad \forall\,b \in \mathcal{B},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:no_sim_ch_dis}
\end{align}
Crucially, the BESS discharge power allocation coupling between bulk energy discharge and virtual inertia headroom (Image~4) is enforced:
\begin{align}
  P_{b,t}^{\text{dis}} + \frac{P_{b,t}^{\text{VI}}}{\eta_b^{\text{VI}}} &\leq DR_b^{\max}\,u_{b,t}^{\text{dis}} \quad \forall\,b \in \mathcal{B},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:bess_coupled_discharge} \\
  P_{b,t}^{\text{VI}} &\geq 0 \quad \forall\,b \in \mathcal{B},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:vi_nonneg}
\end{align}
where $u_{b,t}^{\text{ch}}, u_{b,t}^{\text{dis}} \in \{0, 1\}$ prevent simultaneous charging and discharging, and $CR_b^{\max}, DR_b^{\max}$ are converter power ratings [MW].

\subsubsection{C6: Renewable Curtailment Limits}
Curtailment for wind and solar PV power is non-negative and bounded by instantaneous available generation (Images~2, 3):
\begin{align}
  0 \leq P_{w,t}^{\text{curt}} &\leq P_{w,t}^{\text{avail}} \quad \forall\,w \in \mathcal{W},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:wt_curt_limit} \\
  0 \leq P_{v,t}^{\text{curt}} &\leq P_{v,t}^{\text{avail}} \quad \forall\,v \in \mathcal{V},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:pv_curt_limit}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{C7: System Inertia and RoCoF Security Constraint}
To preserve frequency stability against excessive RoCoF, the aggregate system inertia---consisting of synchronous thermal inertia and BESS virtual inertia---must satisfy the dynamic RoCoF security constraint (Image~4):
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{g=1}^{N} H_g\,P_g^{\max}\,u_{g,t} + \sum_{b=1}^{B} K_b^{\text{VI}}\,P_{b,t}^{\text{VI}} \geq \frac{f_0\,P^{\text{loss}}_t}{2\,\text{RoCoF}^{\max}} \quad \forall\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:rocof_constraint_full}
\end{equation}
where $P^{\text{loss}}_t = \max_{g \in \mathcal{G}} (P_g^{\max}\,u_{g,t})$ is the largest potential outage capacity. Equation~\eqref{eq:rocof_constraint_full} explicitly links BESS virtual inertia headroom to system frequency security, with wind and solar excluded from virtual inertia provision.

\subsubsection{C8: Primary Frequency Regulation (PFR) Constraints}
Following the PFR formulation in~\cite{E02Sulawesi}, primary reserve headroom, ramp limits, contingency adequacy, and steady-state frequency bounds are enforced:
\begin{align}
  0 \leq R_{g,t}^{\text{PFR}} &\leq P_g^{\max}\,u_{g,t} - P_{g,t} \quad \forall\,g \in \mathcal{G},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:pfr_headroom_con} \\
  R_{g,t}^{\text{PFR}} &\leq R_g^{\text{PFR},\max}\,u_{g,t} \quad \forall\,g \in \mathcal{G},\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:pfr_ramp_con} \\
  \sum_{g \neq k} R_{g,t}^{\text{PFR}} &\geq P_{k_t} \quad \forall\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:pfr_adequacy} \\
  \Delta f_t &\geq \Delta f_{\text{ss}}^{\min} \quad \forall\,t \in \mathcal{T}
  \label{eq:pfr_dft_limit}
\end{align}

\subsection{MILP Linearization of Primary Frequency Regulation}

The governor droop characteristic~\eqref{eq:pfr_piecewise} features a piecewise non-smooth relationship and bilinear products between commitment status $u_{g,t}$ and frequency deviation $\Delta f_t$. To solve the model using standard Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) solvers such as IBM CPLEX, we implement the exact linearization method developed by Restrepo and Galiana~\cite{Restrepo2005} and applied in the Southern Sulawesi model~\cite{E02Sulawesi}.

The linearization employs two binary indicator variables and two continuous slack variables:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $V_{g,t} \in \{0, 1\}$: indicates whether available unit capacity headroom is smaller than the primary ramp capability ($V_{g,t} = 1$) or the primary ramp limit binds ($V_{g,t} = 0$).
  \item $X_{g,t} \in \{0, 1\}$: indicates whether the generator operates on its proportional droop response slope ($X_{g,t} = 0$) or reaches primary reserve saturation ($X_{g,t} = 1$).
  \item $S_{g,t} = V_{g,t}\,P_{g,t}$: continuous McCormick bilinear product between $V_{g,t}$ and dispatched generation $P_{g,t}$.
  \item $H_{g,t} = u_{g,t}\,\Delta f_t$: continuous bilinear product between commitment status $u_{g,t}$ and steady-state frequency deviation $\Delta f_t$.
\end{itemize}

The ceiling power output $P_{g,t}^{\max,\text{FR}} = \min(P_g^{\max}\,u_{g,t}, P_{g,t} + R_g^{\text{PFR},\max})$ is linearized through:
\begin{align}
  P_{g,t}^{\max,\text{FR}} &= P_g^{\max}\,u_{g,t} + (R_g^{\text{PFR},\max} - P_g^{\max})\,V_{g,t} + S_{g,t}
  \label{eq:lin_pmaxfr} \\
  P_g^{\min}\,V_{g,t} &\leq S_{g,t} \leq P_g^{\max}\,V_{g,t}
  \label{eq:lin_slack1} \\
  P_{g,t} - P_g^{\max}\,(1 - V_{g,t}) &\leq S_{g,t} \leq P_{g,t}
  \label{eq:lin_slack2}
\end{align}

The binary saturation indicator $X_{g,t}$ is governed by:
\begin{align}
  -\frac{\Delta f_t}{R_g^{\text{PFR},\max}} + \frac{\Delta f_{g,t}^{\max}}{R_g^{\text{PFR},\max}} &\leq X_{g,t} \leq 1 + \frac{\Delta f_t}{R_g^{\text{PFR},\max}} - \frac{\Delta f_{g,t}^{\max}}{R_g^{\text{PFR},\max}}
  \label{eq:lin_xflag}
\end{align}
The delivered primary reserve $R_{g,t}^{\text{PFR}}$ is bounded linearly by:
\begin{align}
  -K_g\,H_{g,t} - P_g^{\max}\,X_{g,t} &\leq R_{g,t}^{\text{PFR}} \leq -K_g\,H_{g,t}
  \label{eq:lin_rpfr1} \\
  -K_g\,\Delta f_{g,t}^{\max} - P_g^{\max}\,(1 - X_{g,t}) &\leq R_{g,t}^{\text{PFR}} \leq -K_g\,\Delta f_{g,t}^{\max}
  \label{eq:lin_rpfr2}
\end{align}
Finally, the bilinear product $H_{g,t} = u_{g,t}\,\Delta f_t$ is linearized via McCormick envelopes over the physical domain $\Delta f_t \in [\Delta f_{\text{ss}}^{\min}, 0]$:
\begin{align}
  \Delta f_{\text{ss}}^{\min}\,u_{g,t} &\leq H_{g,t} \leq 0
  \label{eq:lin_h1} \\
  \Delta f_t - \Delta f_{\text{ss}}^{\min}\,(1 - u_{g,t}) &\leq H_{g,t} \leq \Delta f_t
  \label{eq:lin_h2}
\end{align}

The complete FCUC model comprising objective~\eqref{eq:objective_full} subject to constraints C1--C8 (\eqref{eq:power_balance}--\eqref{eq:lin_h2}) is a fully linear \textbf{Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP)}, guaranteeing global optimality without nonlinear solver complications.

\subsection{Baseline Classical UC Without Frequency Constraints (S1)}

Scenario~1 establishes the cost baseline at \$4{,}029{,}223/day.
The worst-case estimated RoCoF is \textbf{0.903~Hz/s}---64\% above
the 0.55~Hz/s limit---with violations in \textbf{all 24} scheduling
periods. Average system inertia is only 36{,}623~MWs, with a
minimum of 32{,}565~MWs during off-peak hours. At this inertia level,
the loss of the largest committed unit causes RoCoF that would
trigger relay operation in practical systems. This confirms that
classical UC is frequency-insecure under N-1 contingency conditions.

\subsection{BESS and Wind Turbine Integration Without Frequency Constraints (S2, S3)}

\textbf{Scenario~2} (classical UC + BESS) achieves a marginal
cost reduction of only 0.23\% versus S1 (\$4{,}019{,}885/day).
The BESS reduces startup costs from \$61{,}840 to \$5{,}712 by
smoothing commitment scheduling, but slightly increases fuel cost.
Frequency security remains violated in all 24 hours.

\textbf{Scenario~3} (classical UC + BESS + WT) achieves the
\emph{greatest single cost reduction} of \textbf{7.76\%}
versus S1, reaching \$3{,}716{,}391/day. Wind generation
(400~MW) reduces thermal fuel consumption by \$226{,}220/day.
Startup costs fall to \$2{,}592. Zero wind curtailment is
observed, indicating full wind utilization. However,
frequency security remains violated in all 24 hours.

\subsection{Enforcing RoCoF Constraint via SG Inertia Only (S4, S5)}

\textbf{Scenario~4} (FCUC + BESS + minimum inertia constraint only,
no WT) requires additional must-run thermal generators to satisfy
constraint~\eqref{eq:rocof_con}, increasing total cost to
\$4{,}176{,}358/day ($+$3.65\% vs.\ S1).
Fixed costs rise from \$514{,}954 to \$555{,}881 due to
additional committed units, while startup costs vanish (units
run continuously).
All 24 hours satisfy RoCoF~$\leq$~0.55~Hz/s with an
average $H_{\text{sys}}$ of 42{,}980~MWs.

\textbf{Scenario~5} (FCUC + BESS + WT + minimum inertia) demonstrates
that adding wind generation \emph{while enforcing} frequency security
recovers significant cost: \$3{,}944{,}158/day ($-$5.57\% vs.\ S4,
$-$2.11\% vs.\ S1). Wind reduces fuel cost by \$232{,}200/day
versus S4. Minor wind curtailment of 1.3\% appears as the optimizer
balances wind output against reserve and inertia requirements.

\subsection{Virtual Inertia Contribution (S6, S7)}

\textbf{Scenario~6} activates BESS virtual inertia
(\texttt{con\_battery\_vi}), contributing an average of
\textbf{236.5~MWs} of fast synthetic inertia via grid-forming converter
control with explicit discharge power allocation headroom.
This eliminates all RoCoF violations across the 24-hour horizon,
achieving a total operating cost of \$3{,}938{,}983/day (a $-$5.70\%
reduction versus Scenario~4 and 2.24\% below the unconstrained
classical UC baseline).

\textbf{Scenario~7} comparatively explores activating both WT
virtual inertia (\texttt{con\_wt\_vi}) and QSS constraint
(\texttt{con\_qss\_limit}). BESS-VI supplies \textbf{263.5~MWs},
while WT-VI contributes a minor \textbf{12.7~MWs} of additional inertia
(less than 0.03\% of total system inertia), with a total cost of
\$3{,}939{,}091/day (+\$108 relative to S6).
While WT kinetic extraction is technically possible from curtailment headroom,
it introduces aerodynamic and mechanical rotor stress as well as post-contingency
frequency recovery dip risks. Consequently, allocating virtual inertia
strictly to dedicated BESS (as formulated in Section~\ref{sec:model})
while maintaining wind turbines and solar PV as pure energy producers
represents the most reliable and physically robust operational strategy.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper proposed, implemented, and validated a MILP-based
Frequency-Constrained Unit Commitment model incorporating
dedicated BESS virtual inertia, governor-droop Primary Frequency
Regulation, and renewable generation from wind turbines and solar PV,
solved using Python~3.9/Pyomo and IBM CPLEX~22.1.1.
Seven simulation scenarios on a modified IEEE 10-unit test system
yielded the following conclusions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Classical UC is frequency-insecure.} Scenario~1
    shows a worst-case RoCoF of 0.903~Hz/s (limit: 0.55~Hz/s)
    violated in all 24 hours, confirming the necessity of FCUC.

  \item \textbf{Renewable integration provides significant cost reduction.}
    Scenario~3 achieves $-$7.76\% versus S1 through fuel cost savings
    of \$226{,}220/day, but does not address dynamic frequency security.

  \item \textbf{RoCoF enforcement via synchronous inertia alone is costly.}
    Scenario~4 incurs $+$3.65\% versus S1, requiring more must-run
    thermal units to satisfy minimum inertia requirements.

  \item \textbf{BESS virtual inertia eliminates the frequency security penalty.}
    Scenario~6 saves 5.70\% versus S4 (\$237{,}375/day) while
    maintaining full RoCoF compliance across all 24 hours through an
    average of 236.5~MWs of BESS virtual inertia, without requiring
    kinetic extraction from wind turbine rotors.

  \item \textbf{Frequency security achievable below baseline cost.}
    Operating with BESS virtual inertia costs 2.24\% \emph{less} than
    classical UC while being fully frequency-secure, demonstrating that
    co-optimizing renewable energy and BESS virtual inertia simultaneously
    reduces operational costs and guarantees grid security.
\end{enumerate}

For Indonesia's RUPTL 2025--2034 goal of 61\% renewable generation,
these results indicate that explicit scheduling of virtual inertia
from planned BESS assets, combined with PFR-aware commitment, can fulfill
inertia and frequency requirements without imposing additional cost burdens.
Future work will extend the model to: (i) stochastic formulation
for renewable forecast uncertainty; (ii) transmission-constrained
(DC power flow) FCUC; and (iii) validation on actual Indonesian
power system topology (JAMALI or Sulawesi grid).
